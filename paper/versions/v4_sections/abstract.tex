A token that states a value in context, such as \emph{shelf} in ``the candle is moved to the shelf'', can be read by later tokens through its attention key, which a later query matches, or its value, which is copied. We clamp the two separately at this writing token in \nModels{} instruction-tuned models from four families. When the candidates are mentioned after the writing token, by an option list or, from 7B up, a neutral sentence, the value's identity is looked up through the key; otherwise it is mainly copied, even when the same list precedes it. At Qwen2.5-7B and Mistral-7B, 5\,\% of attention heads, whose core is canonical duplicate-token heads, read the writing token's key at the option words (\HoneRQwenSeven{} and \HoneRMistralSeven{} of the effect; random heads \HtwoRrandQwenSeven{} and \HtwoRrandMistralSeven{}), and the answer reads the option words directly. Such attention is also present at 1.5B and 3B under the sentence format, where their key identity is about zero. A released learned remap behaves nearly the same in every format, yet whether its keys or values appear to carry it follows the format's readers, also when refit without later mentions; at Mistral-Small-24B a preregistered test traced its key effect to sparse reader heads at the option words, without which its values carry more of the effect but less of it is kept than predicted. On Prakash et al.'s binding swap, replicated at Qwen2.5-14B, a preregistered law predicting its key share from the natural read at that depth, which carries almost no key identity, failed: the swap is key-dominant in every evaluable format (in two, a margin shift that flips no answer), and later mentions shift its key share less than predicted. We mark exploratory results and report every failed prediction.
